Restoring Fish Passage in the Fraser Region - 2025

Tabor Creek - 196085 & 203582 - Appendix

Site Location

PSCIS crossings 196085 and 203582 are located on Tabor Creek , approximately 8km south of Prince George, BC, in the Tabor River watershed group (Figure 5.22). Crossing 196085 is located 1.4km upstream Tabor Creek’s confluence with the Fraser River. The crossing is located on Willow-Cale FSR and is the responsibility of the Ministry of Transportation and Infrastructure (chris_culvert_id: 31046). Approximately 400m upstream, crossing 203582 is located on and the responsibility of the CN Railway.

Background

At the location of these crossings, Tabor Creek is a fifth order stream and drains a watershed of approximately 145.6km2. The watershed ranges in elevation from a maximum of 1254m to 592m near the crossing (Table 5.22).


Crossing 196085 on Willow-Cale FSR was previously assessed in 2014 by Hooft (2015) and ranked as a barrier to fish passage, with medium value habitat documented and fish observed at the time of assessment. Chinook were recorded downstream of the crossing in 2004, and upstream of the crossing rainbow trout and dolly varden have been previously documented (Norris [2018] 2024; MoE 2024a). Crossing 203582 on the CN Railway had not been assessed prior to this project. Barrier assessments were completed at both crossings, and a habitat confirmation encompassing both sites was conducted.


A summary of habitat modelling outputs for the crossing are presented in Table 5.23.

Stream Characteristics Downstream of Crossing 196085

The stream was surveyed downstream from crossing 196085 for 350m (Figure 5.23). The stream was flowing, but discharge appeared minimal relative to channel-forming flows. Extensive algae growth near the highway suggested potential nutrient inputs, possibly from the upstream mill site; algae presence decreased farther downstream (Figure 5.24).. Large areas of gravels were present and suitable for chinook and sockeye spawning, contingent on adequate flows during spawning periods. Total cover amount was rated as trace with boulders dominant. Cover was also present as undercut banks and instream vegetation.The dominant substrate was boulders with cobbles sub-dominant.The average channel width was 9.9m, the average wetted width was 3.4m, and the average gradient was 2%. The habitat was rated as medium value.


Stream Characteristics Upstream of Crossing 196085 and Downstream of Crossing 203582

The stream was surveyed upstream from crossing 196085 for 410m , up to crossing 203582 (Figure 5.25). The habitat was rated as medium value. The average channel width was 11m, the average wetted width was 1.9m, and the average gradient was 1.5%.Total cover amount was rated as trace with boulders dominant. Cover was also present as undercut banks.The dominant substrate was gravels with cobbles sub-dominant. The channel was dewatered at the time of assessment with shallow pools present at the downstream end of the site. Gravels were abundant throughout and suitable for chinook and sockeye spawning under adequate flow conditions. Deep pools were likely present when the stream was flowing.


Stream Characteristics Upstream of Crossing 203582

The stream was surveyed upstream from crossing 203582 for 650m (Figure 5.26). The habitat was rated as medium value. The stream was dewatered until approximately 360m upstream of the railway culverts, and the farthest downstream pool contained numerous fry. Upstream, occasional pools transitioned to increasing flow toward the upper end of the site where a continually flowing channel reemerged. The system was extremely large with high flows during freshet based on the size of the substrates. Gravels were abundant throughout, with limited woody debris or in stream cover at low flow, restricted mainly to interstitial spaces between cobbles and boulders. Total cover amount was rated as trace with boulders dominant. Cover was also present as undercut banks.The average channel width was 14.6m, the average wetted width was 2.5m, and the average gradient was 0.8%.The dominant substrate was gravels with cobbles sub-dominant.

Structure Remediation and Cost Estimate

Should restoration/maintenance activities proceed, replacement of the Willow-Cale FSR crossing (196085) with a bridge (18m span) is recommended. At the time of reporting in 2025, the cost of the work is estimated at $3,600,000.


Should restoration/maintenance activities proceed at the CN Railway crossing (203582) replacement with a bridge (36 m span) is recommended. At the time of reporting in 2025, the cost of the work is estimated at $27,000,000.


Conclusion

Tabor Creek provided medium value habitat for salmonid spawning and rearing, with chinook documented downstream of the Willow-Cale FSR crossing. Due to previous remediation work completed at this site, the crossing is ranked as a moderate priority for replacement. The upstream crossing on the CN Railway is ranked as a moderate priority for replacement due to a 0.3m outlet drop. Replacement would be costly because the structure is located on the railway, and the stream exhibited sections of dewatering above Willow-Cale FSR; however, flow was regained upstream and evidence of high flows was observed. Both crossings are situated near the Fraser River and are in close proximity to the City of Prince George, offering opportunities for community engagement.


The channel above the railway crossings was dewatered at the time of assessment, with the farthest downstream residual pool holding numerous fry and environmental DNA confirming chinook salmon and rainbow trout immediately upstream. Reaches that go dry are easily read as low value, and the literature points the other way. Residual pools within intermittent systems can support high juvenile growth rates and elevated overwinter survival, and both Ebersole et al. (2006) and Wigington Jr et al. (2006) measured smolts emerging from intermittent tributaries as considerably larger than those rearing in perennial habitat, with correspondingly greater survival — outcomes attributed to lower competitive densities and greater food availability (Maslin and McKinnev 1998). That work concerns coho salmon, so its application to the chinook rearing above the Tabor Creek railway crossings is an extension rather than a direct read-across, but the situation is the same one those studies describe.


If that is what is occurring here, the value of the reach depends on whether those fish can leave it when flows resume, which makes the timing of connectivity the question worth monitoring rather than the 0.3 m outlet drop considered on its own. Tracking flow resumption, pool persistence and fish movement across a season would establish whether the reach is producing large, high-survival migrants or stranding a cohort each year. Where fish are found to be trapped, annual salvage is available as a short-term measure. Confirmation that chinook depend on this reach would also give the Canadian National Railway a concrete basis for considering remediation of the railway crossings.
